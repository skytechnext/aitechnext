§ 01

The Implementation Decision Matrix

Every candidate build — client project, internal tool, or product idea — gets scored on the same ten factors before anyone writes code. The matrix doesn't make the decision; it forces the argument into the open and makes "no" cheap to say early.

Verdicts and hard gates

Verdict bands (weighted score, 1–5)

≥ 4.0 · Commit — schedule it into a pod this or next sprint.
3.2–3.9 · Pilot — scope down to the smallest valuable slice, time-box it, re-score after.
2.4–3.1 · Backlog — park with a written trigger that would change the score.
< 2.4 · Decline — say no now, kindly and in writing.

Gates that override the score

Legal ≤ 2 → hold, whatever the total; route through the governance board (dossier §16) first.
Team readiness = 1 → enablement first: the project becomes a training item until readiness ≥ 3.
No named owner → no build. A score without a sponsor on each side is a wish, not a decision.

How to use it, practically

Manager and CAIO score independently (10 minutes each), then compare. Any factor where you differ by ≥2 points is the conversation — the matrix's real job is surfacing that gap. Record the final row in the decision log (§06); revisit Backlog items monthly and Pilots at their time-box.

The leadership interview — 12 questions (v1)

  1. Where does the business lose the most hours to repetitive work — and who exactly is losing them?
  2. Which decisions wait on information that is slow or painful to assemble?
  3. What do people re-type from one system into another, and how often?
  4. Which three questions do customers ask most, and how long do they wait for answers?
  5. What does the owner still personally check, approve, or chase every week?
  6. Which AI tools are already in use — officially, and unofficially on personal accounts?
  7. What has been tried with AI so far, and what happened to it afterwards?
  8. If your most experienced person resigned tomorrow, what knowledge leaves with them?
  9. Where does revenue leak — quotes unfollowed, enquiries gone cold, renewals missed?
  10. What systems do you pay for that nobody fully uses — and why did adoption stall?
  11. What would you automate first if it were guaranteed to work, and what has stopped you?
  12. How will we both know, in 90 days, that this engagement worked? (The answer becomes the baseline.)

The adoption checklist — 24 checks, scored (v1)

One point per "yes". 0–8 = Ad-hoc (start with foundations) · 9–16 = Emerging (systematize the wins) · 17–24 = Operational (scale and govern). The band, not the number, drives the roadmap's first quarter.

  • Leadership & strategy
  • A named executive owns the AI agenda (not "everyone" — someone).
  • A written list of AI use cases exists, ranked by value.
  • AI spend is a budget line, not hidden inside personal expense claims.
  • Leadership has used an AI tool hands-on in the last 30 days.
  • There is a stated position on what AI must never be used for here.
  • Data & systems
  • One system of record for customers/orders exists and is trusted.
  • Core SOPs are written down somewhere findable (not only in heads).
  • Key systems expose APIs or exports that the team can actually access.
  • Customer/product records are reasonably deduplicated.
  • Someone can produce last month's key numbers without manual assembly.
  • Files live in shared, permissioned storage — not personal desktops.
  • People & usage
  • More than half the team has used an AI tool for work this month.
  • At least one person per department is a recognized go-to for AI help.
  • Prompt patterns or templates are shared, not reinvented per person.
  • Any AI output that reaches customers gets a human review step.
  • Someone has been trained (formally, at all) on AI tooling this year.
  • New-hire onboarding mentions the tools the team actually uses.
  • Governance & hygiene
  • An acceptable-use rule exists: what data may enter which tools.
  • Company accounts (not personal) are used for AI tools handling work data.
  • Access to systems is revoked promptly when someone leaves.
  • Passwords live in a manager, not spreadsheets or chat threads.
  • Customer consent posture is known for the data AI would touch.
  • At least one automation, if any exist, is monitored — not fire-and-forget.
  • Someone would notice within a day if an automation silently failed.

§ 06

Operating Cadence & Decision Log

The matrix only works if it runs on a rhythm. Thirty minutes weekly, one hour monthly — and every verdict written down, so future-us can audit past-us.

Weekly · 30 min · Manager + CAIO

Intake & score

New candidates from pipeline, pods, and complaints land here. Score independently before the meeting; argue only the ≥2-point gaps; log the verdict. Standing agenda item: any Pilot hitting its time-box gets re-scored or killed — no silent extensions.

Monthly · 60 min · + CEO

Stack review

Walk the P1–P10 stack against milestones and KPIs; re-score anything whose facts changed; promote from Backlog only by re-score, never by enthusiasm. Output: the updated stack published back to this page.

The two failure modes this page exists to prevent

Building what's exciting instead of what scores — the matrix makes enthusiasm argue with evidence. And the quiet yes — work that starts because nobody said no. If it isn't in the decision log, it doesn't get engineering hours. No exceptions, including for the people who own this page.
